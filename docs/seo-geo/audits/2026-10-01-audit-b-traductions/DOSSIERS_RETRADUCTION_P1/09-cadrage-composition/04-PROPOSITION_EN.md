# Product photography composition: framing rules that help you sell

[`h1` actuel : « Photo tips: framing and composing to sell ». Voir `05` et `06`, point 3.]

Before you even think about framing or composition, ask yourself one essential question: **why are you taking this photo?** In other words, **what do you want to show, tell or make people feel?**\
That **intent** will guide your technical and creative choices throughout the shoot. This is even more true in **product photography**, where every image has to **grab attention**, **build trust**… and **sell**.

## 1. Intent over technique: define your visual message

Here are a few simple questions to guide your approach:

- **What is the main subject?**
- **What context** should you include or leave out?
- Should you **highlight details** or **show the whole scene**?
- **What should the viewer feel** when looking at this image?

![The same pair of blue lace-up boots photographed twice: on a plain blue background, then on a light background with a green leaf and sunglasses](/images/blog/67e40e909cefed03b0448611.avif)

A few concrete examples:

- A luxury piece of jewelry with a fine finish? → **Fill the frame** to show off the material.
- A pair of shoes in a lifestyle setting? → **Leave space** around them to tell a story.
- A bag being worn? → Think about the **relationship between the subject, the wearer and the viewer's gaze**.

## 2. Fill the frame or leave space: find the right balance for your product

One of the first composition choices is deciding on the **relationship between the subject and the space** around it. A few useful guidelines:

**Fill the frame** if:

- The subject is **rare, complex or rich in detail**
- You want to create a **strong connection with the viewer**
- You want to **maximize visual impact** in a small space (for example, e-commerce thumbnails)

**Don't fill the frame** if:

- The **setting** or the **styling** is what gives the image its meaning
- You want to **suggest a world or a mood**
- The subject's **movement** or **gaze** needs room to express itself

## 3. Composition that serves your product

➤ **Off-center the subject.** It's a basic rule of photography: avoid placing the subject in the center of the frame. A subject that is too centered often looks static, even frozen.

➤ **Leave space around the subject.** Don't push the object up against the edges of the frame. Give it room to breathe, especially if it is moving or “looking” in a particular direction.

➤ **Stay off the central axes.** Unless you are deliberately going for that look, avoid lining up key elements on the vertical or horizontal axis that runs through the center of the frame.

## 4. The rule of thirds: position your products strategically

The **rule of thirds** is a classic composition method inherited from painting. It involves dividing your image into nine equal parts with two horizontal and two vertical lines.\
These are the **thirds lines**, and the **points where they intersect** are called **power points**. They are natural places to position the subject or key elements.

![Rule of thirds diagram: a frame divided into nine equal parts, with the thirds lines and the power points where they intersect](/images/blog/67dbae6aaff27501162f332a.avif)

Examples:

- In landscape photography, place the horizon on the **lower or upper third**, never in the center.
- For an eyewear packshot, position the lenses on one of the **thirds lines** rather than right in the center of the image.
- For a hand-held product, place the **model's eyes** on one of the power points. [À VALIDER : même point que le FR, `06`, point 6.]

## 5. Create movement: bring your packshots to life

Even in a still packshot, it's important to **leave space in front of the subject's gaze or movement**. This creates **visual momentum**.\
Whether the movement is **real** (a step, a rotation) or **implied** (a glance, an orientation), it needs room to play out in the image.

## Conclusion: rules… or guidelines?

The points we've just covered aren't fixed laws but **guidelines** to help you structure your shots. Photography is also a matter of **instinct**. If another composition looks more **aesthetically pleasing** or **truer to your visual message**, go with it.\
What matters is staying **consistent** with your intent, with the **perception** you want to create and with the **end use** of the image.\
So before you think about framing or composition, ask yourself why you're taking this photo: what do you want to show, tell or make people feel?

## FAQ

### What is the ideal distance to photograph a product?

The ideal distance depends on the product and your goal. For small items whose details you need to show (jewelry, watches), move in close with a macro lens. For larger products (clothing, furniture), step back far enough to capture the whole item while keeping good definition. The key is to keep the important elements sharp and avoid distortion.

### Should you always use a white background for product photography?

No. A white background is the standard on e-commerce sites, but other options can showcase your product. Choose the background based on your target audience and the mood you want: a contextual background can tell a story, while a plain colored background can create a strong visual identity. What matters is consistency across your catalog.

### How do you avoid unwanted reflections on shiny products?

For shiny products (jewelry, glassware, metal objects), use diffused lighting with softboxes or umbrellas. Place your lights at 45° to the product and use reflectors to control shadows. For highly reflective surfaces, you may need a light tent. A polarizing filter can also reduce some specular reflections. [À VALIDER : même point que le FR, `06`, point 7.]

### What camera settings should I use for product photography?

Use a low ISO (100 to 200) to maximize image quality. Choose a medium aperture (f/8 to f/11) for good depth of field. Shutter speed matters less if you're using a tripod. In manual mode, set your white balance to match your lighting for accurate colors. Shoot in RAW for more flexibility in post-processing.

### What is the difference between packshot photography and lifestyle product photography?

Packshot photography focuses on the product on its own, usually against a neutral background, to show its features and details clearly. It is essential for e-commerce product pages. Lifestyle photography, on the other hand, shows the product in use, in a real-life setting or environment. It lets you tell a story, evoke a lifestyle and show what the product does. A complete visual strategy often combines both approaches: packshots to inform, lifestyle images to inspire and build an emotional connection.
